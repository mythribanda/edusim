# Definition

## Concise Meaning

Gravity is a fundamental natural phenomenon by which all things with mass or energy—including planets, stars, galaxies, and even light—are attracted to one another. It is one of the four fundamental interactions of nature, alongside the strong interaction, the weak interaction, and electromagnetism. In everyday experience, gravity is most familiar as the force that gives weight to physical objects and causes them to fall to the ground when dropped.

# Key Points

## Important Highlights

*   **Universal Attraction:** Gravity is an attractive force, meaning it always pulls objects together; it never pushes them apart.
*   **Mass Dependence:** The strength of the gravitational force between two objects is directly proportional to the product of their masses. More massive objects exert a stronger gravitational pull.
*   **Distance Dependence:** The strength of the gravitational force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between the centers of the two objects. This means that as objects move farther apart, the gravitational force between them weakens rapidly.
*   **Weakest Fundamental Force:** Compared to the strong nuclear force, the weak nuclear force, and electromagnetism, gravity is by far the weakest of the four fundamental forces. However, its long range and universal attraction make it dominant on astronomical scales.
*   **Action at a Distance:** Gravity acts over vast distances, holding planets in orbit around stars and stars within galaxies.
*   **Curvature of Spacetime (General Relativity):** According to Einstein's theory of General Relativity, gravity is not a force in the traditional sense but rather a manifestation of the curvature of spacetime caused by the presence of mass and energy. Objects follow the shortest path (geodesics) through this curved spacetime, which we perceive as gravitational attraction.

# Characteristics

*   **Always Attractive:** Gravity only pulls objects together; it never repels them. This is a key distinction from electromagnetic forces, which can be both attractive and repulsive.
*   **Long-Range Force:** The gravitational force has an infinite range, meaning it theoretically acts between any two objects with mass, no matter how far apart they are. However, its strength diminishes rapidly with distance.
*   **Non-Contact Force:** Gravity acts without direct physical contact between objects. A planet orbits a star without touching it, held by the star's gravitational pull.
*   **Universal:** Every particle of matter in the universe attracts every other particle. This universality is a cornerstone of Newton's law of universal gravitation.
*   **Weak Force:** On a microscopic scale, gravity is exceedingly weak compared to other fundamental forces. For instance, the electromagnetic force between two electrons is vastly stronger than their gravitational attraction. Its effects become significant only when dealing with large masses, like planets or stars.
*   **Conservative Force:** The work done by gravity on an object moving between two points is independent of the path taken. This implies that a potential energy can be associated with the gravitational field.

# Formula

The gravitational force between two point masses is described by Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation:

$$
F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}
$$

Where:
*   $F$ represents the magnitude of the gravitational force between the two objects (measured in Newtons, N).
*   $G$ represents the gravitational constant (approximately $6.674 \times 10^{-11} \text{ N} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$).
*   $m_1$ represents the mass of the first object (measured in kilograms, kg).
*   $m_2$ represents the mass of the second object (measured in kilograms, kg).
*   $r$ represents the distance between the centers of the two objects (measured in meters, m).

# Derivation

Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation was not derived from more fundamental principles in the same way that some laws are derived from axioms. Instead, it was formulated by Isaac Newton based on empirical observations and mathematical reasoning, particularly from analyzing the motion of planets and the fall of objects on Earth.

The key steps in Newton's reasoning included:

1.  **Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion:** Johannes Kepler had previously formulated three empirical laws describing planetary orbits:
    *   Planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus.
    *   A line segment joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time.
    *   The square of the orbital period of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit ($T^2 \propto r^3$).

2.  **Inverse Square Law:** Newton deduced that for planets to follow Kepler's third law, the force attracting them to the Sun must decrease with the square of the distance from the Sun. He also considered the acceleration of the Moon towards the Earth and compared it to the acceleration of objects falling on Earth, finding a consistent inverse square relationship.

3.  **Proportionality to Mass:** Newton reasoned that if an apple falls to the Earth due to gravity, then the Earth must also be attracted to the apple. By his third law of motion (action-reaction), these forces must be equal in magnitude. This led him to conclude that the gravitational force must be proportional to the mass of both interacting objects.

4.  **Universal Constant:** To turn the proportionality into an equality, a constant of proportionality, $G$, was introduced. This constant, known as the universal gravitational constant, accounts for the strength of gravity and makes the equation dimensionally consistent.

Combining these insights, Newton proposed the universal law of gravitation, stating that every particle attracts every other particle in the universe with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centers.

# Applications

*   **Planetary Orbits:** Gravity is responsible for keeping planets in orbit around stars, moons in orbit around planets, and artificial satellites in orbit around Earth.
*   **Tides:** The gravitational pull of the Moon and, to a lesser extent, the Sun causes the Earth's ocean tides.
*   **Formation of Celestial Bodies:** Gravity is the primary force responsible for the formation of stars, planets, and galaxies by drawing together interstellar gas and dust.
*   **Weight:** The weight of an object on Earth is the force of gravity exerted by the Earth on that object.
*   **Atmospheric Retention:** Earth's gravity holds its atmosphere in place, which is crucial for life.
*   **Space Exploration:** Understanding gravity is essential for launching rockets, maneuvering spacecraft, and planning interplanetary missions.
*   **Navigation:** Gravitational anomalies can affect navigation systems, and precise measurements of gravity are used in geodesy and surveying.
*   **Black Holes:** Gravity is so intense in black holes that nothing, not even light, can escape their pull once it crosses the event horizon.

# Advantages

*   **Predictability of Celestial Motion:** Newton's law of gravitation allows for highly accurate predictions of the movements of planets, moons, and other celestial bodies, which is crucial for astronomy and space exploration.
*   **Foundation for Modern Physics:** The study of gravity led to Einstein's theory of General Relativity, which revolutionized our understanding of space, time, and the universe.
*   **Essential for Life:** Gravity holds atmospheres around planets, retains water, and provides a stable environment for life to thrive.
*   **Structural Stability:** On Earth, gravity provides a downward force that helps stabilize structures and allows for the design of buildings and infrastructure.
*   **Energy Generation (Hydroelectric):** Gravity is harnessed in hydroelectric power plants, where the potential energy of water at higher elevations is converted into kinetic energy to drive turbines.

---

# Disadvantages

*   **Requires Significant Energy for Space Travel:** Overcoming Earth's gravitational pull requires immense amounts of energy, making space launches expensive and challenging.
*   **Causes Stress on Structures:** Gravity exerts constant stress on buildings, bridges, and other structures, requiring robust engineering to prevent collapse.
*   **Physiological Effects on Astronauts:** Prolonged exposure to microgravity in space can lead to bone density loss, muscle atrophy, and other adverse health effects for astronauts.
*   **Limits Object Size:** For very large objects, gravity can cause them to collapse under their own weight, limiting the maximum size of mountains or even organisms.
*   **Challenges in Precision Measurements:** Gravitational forces, though weak, can interfere with highly sensitive scientific experiments, requiring careful isolation and compensation.

# Summary

Gravity is a fundamental force of nature that governs the attraction between all objects possessing mass or energy. Described by Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation, it states that the force of attraction is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. While the weakest of the four fundamental forces, its long range and universal nature make it dominant on cosmic scales, responsible for planetary orbits, the formation of stars and galaxies, and the phenomenon of weight on Earth. Einstein's General Relativity provides a more profound understanding, describing gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy. Gravity is indispensable for the existence of celestial structures and life as we know it, yet it also presents challenges, particularly in space exploration and the design of large structures.
